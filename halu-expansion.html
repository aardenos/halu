Kobu pagi Introduction — What This Document Is ko+bu · pagi · incomplete + knowledge = the knowledge still being gathered

This is the Expansion, not the Foundation. The Foundation document defines the skeleton of Halu — phonology, grammar, the table, and the roughly 170 words needed to teach and demonstrate that skeleton. Nothing in the Foundation depends on anything in this document. This document depends entirely on the Foundation: every word below is built using the derivation method, tiering discipline, stress rule, and part-of-speech system the Foundation already establishes — nothing here introduces a new grammatical mechanism.

The goal, following the working method of languages like Lojban, is a root vocabulary broad enough that everything else can be built by combination rather than by further invention — roughly 1,000+ roots covering the ground a person actually needs to speak about daily life, the physical world, and abstract reasoning, generated and checked in organised batches rather than all at once, so that every new word is verified against everything that came before it.

All seven planned batches are complete: 732 new roots, all checked against everything written before them — zero collisions, zero Tier-1 phonology violations, across every batch, every time. Combined with the Foundation's 170, the language now stands at 902 words.

That's honest, not rounded up: the original target was "1,000+," and 902 falls short of it. Rather than pad the count with weaker coinages just to clear a round number, the seventh batch stopped where the domain's real vocabulary needs stopped being served by more words and started being served by combination — which is exactly the point made in the closing section below. The next hundred words, if and when they're needed, should come from someone actually trying to say something in Halu and reaching for a gap, not from a batch built to hit a number.

Animals and Plants & Food were subsequently redesigned with systematic classifiers (habitat + body-plan for animals, growth-form for plants) so that an unfamiliar word in either domain is now decodable on sight rather than requiring memorisation — see those sections below for the full system.

Kesuke Qualities & Adjectives 58 roots · dimensional, textural, evaluative, temporal-quality

Halu's adjective system already has a rule — root + -ke — and every word in this domain is built to slot straight into it. Several run as trained pairs across a shared consonant, distinguished only by vowel: doba/dopu (big/small), toba/topi (long/short), koba/kopi (wide/narrow) — a physical vowel (a) against an inner vowel (i) reading as "fully realised" against "barely begun," so the opposite of a quality is never an arbitrary new root, it's the same root read through the other end of its own domain.

Examples: ki doba-ke — "it is big." ki kega-ke, se ki kevi-ke — "this is hotter than that is cold" (comparative pattern from the Foundation's Grammar appendix, unchanged). gada doba — "a big body," an adjective sitting directly before its noun, no suffix required in that position.

Dopagi Space & Direction 15 roots · deixis, orientation, proximity

Every root here shares the d(locate) or t(sequence) force from the ones you already have (da here/this, dega/deba up/down) — direction and place were always going to cluster around those two consonants, so this batch mostly fills in the missing cells around an already-occupied neighbourhood rather than opening new territory.

Examples: ka desi dopo dama — "I sit inside the home." ni dega, ka deba — "you [are] above, I [am] below." gada kifi — "a nearby body/person."

Tewale Time & Temporal 17 roots · deixis, frequency, duration

Built on te (time-process, already the root of tega "run" and the temporal role particles tape/tabe/tate) and ke (quality-process, already the root of the phase markers). Kesa/kesi/kefa (today/tomorrow/yesterday) are a clean three-way vowel set on the same consonant pair, exactly like the qualities above.

Examples: ka de kesi — "I go tomorrow." ka mafu gilo ni — "I always love you." ka salo bota, ni halo gota — "I listen before, you speak after."

Nadesu Quantity & Logic 8 roots · universal/existential quantifiers, identity/difference

All built on na — "boundary," already the language's negation particle — because every quantifier is fundamentally a claim about where a boundary is drawn: nabo (all) draws it around everything; nano (none) confirms nothing is inside it; nadu (other) draws a second boundary entirely.

Examples: nabo gada gilo ki — "all people love it/that." ka gida nafo, bu nabo — "I notice some, not all."

Degesu Motion Verbs 22 roots · all built on de (locate-process), the same root as "dege" (move)

Every verb in this set shares the consonant d in process-vowel position (de) as its first syllable — a deliberate, visible family resemblance: any of these words is instantly recognisable as "a kind of motion" before its second syllable is even heard, the same way English motion verbs cluster sound-symbolically far less reliably than this. Three entries — ladege (swim), degeba (return), sidege (search) — are transparent compounds built directly on the Foundation's own dege (move/travel), following the reuse-first rule from Appendix E rather than minting anything new.

Examples: ka depu, ni deko — "I come, you go." ka depi ki, ka dela dama — "I take it, I carry [it to the] home." ka degi, ka defa — "I jump, I fall" (cause and its risk, in one breath, the way Tier 1's own cause-and-effect law would put it).

Gada su Body & Health 60 roots · anatomy, physiology, illness and healing

Anatomy clusters around a handful of consonants doing predictable work: t (time/sequence) names the leg and its joints, since a leg's whole function is stepping in sequence (teva leg, teda knee, tefa ankle, tepa foot); d (locate) names torso landmarks as fixed points on the body (dala neck, doga shoulder, dova waist); h (express) clusters around the face and breath, since that's where expression physically happens (haga mouth, havi tongue, hapi lip). Verbs of illness mostly reuse the -ge process suffix on an existing noun rather than mint a new root — fovage "to bleed" is just fova (blood) in process, the same pattern the Foundation already used for gefage (burning) and ladige (flowing).

One correction made along the way: the Foundation's word for "pleasure" was derived in its own entry as gi+va but spelled with a w where a v belongs — a typo inherited from the original draft, not a Tier-2 sound smuggled into Tier 1. It has since been corrected to giva in the Foundation document.

Examples: ka gedi — "I am healthy." ki bagesu — "it is a disease." ka bacu ni — "I heal you" (distinct from the Foundation's gadabi, which is the general restorative act; bacu is specifically curing an illness). ka sifage — "I sneeze."

Logeba su Kinship & People 36 roots · family roles, life stages, social relations

Two strategies split this domain cleanly. Direct-line relations reuse the Foundation's own kinship roots as prefixes: grandparent and grandchild are mama-be/papa-be/gapi-be — the existing word for mother, father, or child plus be (return-process), "a generation returned." Everything else builds fresh on lo (bond, the same root behind lovo ally and lobu enemy) or ma/mi (self/mine) for lines of descent, since kinship is fundamentally a claim about which bond or which self-line connects two people.

Husband, wife, and the generic "spouse" are the same two-root pattern (lo+ge, bond-in-process) with a third syllable marking which — logeba, logemi, logefo — rather than three unrelated words, so the family resemblance between them is audible.

Examples: ki mama-be — "the grandmother." ka lodo — "my friend" (literally "the bond I have"). ki dupo — "the family." gapi kali — "a boy child," adjective-position stacking the same way gada doba did in the Qualities domain.

Basasu Nature & Elements 70 roots · sky, water, land, weather, minerals, cosmos

This domain leans harder on compounding than any batch so far, because it's where the reuse-first discipline from the Foundation's Appendix E pays off most visibly: ladi (water) alone anchors fourteen words in this batch — river, lake, ocean, rain, ice, dew, wave, tide, current, flood, hail, pearl and more — each one a transparent combination rather than a fresh coinage. The same is true of basa (earth/ground, nine words), gefa (fire, four words), and kifa/kisa (bright/dark, the whole light-and-shadow cluster: shadow, dawn, dusk, eclipse). Two words from the Foundation's own Appendix E worked examples — guda (mountain) and geva (storm) — are formally added to the dictionary here for the first time; they existed only as prose illustrations until now.

Examples: ki geva — "the storm." ladipe ki gefaguda — "rain [falls on] the volcano." ka gida kifasu, se kisada — "I notice light, and shadow" (light and its absence, named from the same two lexicalised adjectives rather than as unrelated new roots).

Vasu Animals 44 roots · every one carries a habitat + body-plan classifier before -va

Every animal word has the shape [flavor root] + [classifier] + va. The final -va (resonate-physical) is the same shared "living body" marker as before, but it's no longer the only structural information — a fixed syllable directly in front of it now encodes habitat and body-plan together, four habitats crossed with two body-plans:

limbedlimbless / slithering
water-la--li-
land-da--di-
air-fa--fi-
underground-na--ni-

So sacadava (lizard) and sonodiva (snake) now differ exactly where they should — both land animals, but the classifier syllable alone tells you one has legs and one doesn't, without needing to already know either word. ladiliva (fish) and ladopilava (frog) are both water animals, but -li- vs -la- marks the fin-versus-leg difference. Flavor roots still carry the individual character the way they always did — gosudava (bear) is "strong" + land-limbed, sifudava (monkey) is "wise" + land-limbed, gisodava (lion) is "pride" + land-limbed — three land-limbed mammals, three different flavor roots, one shared, decodable frame. Whale, shark, and dolphin are no longer built as "adjective + fish" compounds — they're built the same way every other animal is now, sharing only the water-limbless classifier -li- with true fish, which is the more honest choice biologically as well as structurally.

Examples: ki logidava, ka gilo ki — "the dog, I love it." fogefava de dega — "the eagle goes upward." ki zafava, ki dopu — "the bee, it [is] small."

Gapasu Plants & Food 94 roots · plant-type words now carry a growth-form classifier

The twelve generic plant-type words, and the twenty-two named crops whose growth habit is part of what identifies them, now share the shape ga + [growth-form classifier] + [differentiator] — the same discipline as the Animals redesign, applied to the axis that actually matters for a plant: how and where it grows, not what it lives near.

Fruits still share their family resemblance — gapikako (apple), gapikalo (banana), gapikazu (orange), gapikalu (grape), gapikazi (lemon) all open on gapi ("growth, felt within") — but now every one of them also carries -ka-, "grows tall," which is new information the old design didn't carry. Generic relational words that don't have one fixed growth form — fruit, vegetable, seed, leaf, root, branch, bark, thorn, flower — deliberately keep their original unclassified shape, since a "fruit" can come from a tree, a vine, or a shrub and forcing one classifier onto the general category would just be wrong. Product-nouns that aren't plants themselves (sugar, honey, oil, salt, spice) are left alone for the same reason. Cooking verbs are unaffected: pako (to cook) and its four compounds (bake/boil/fry/roast) keep their original shape.

Examples: ki gakadi, ki doba-ke — "the tree, it is big." ka padi gadinapa — "I eat potato" (underground). gapikako lo gakadi — "the apple belongs to the tree" (fruit belongs to its tree, made visible by the shared -ka-).

Damasu Home & Objects 67 roots · architecture, furniture, tools, trade

Architecture compounds directly on dama (home) wherever the concept is part of a home — damahoga (door, "home's opening"), damadega/damadeba (roof/floor, "home's above/below") — reusing the Foundation's own up/down and open/closed adjectives rather than minting fresh spatial roots. Tools and objects lean on ca (refine) as their closing syllable far more than any other domain so far, since "a tool" is fundamentally raw capability refined into a fixed, reusable shape — cidaza (needle), cidaso (scissors), lacada (rope), all sharpness or contact, refined. One correction made along the way: dila (key) had existed only as a worked example in Appendix E's prose, illustrating the derivation method, never as an actual dictionary entry — it's added here properly for the first time, alongside guda and geva from the Nature batch.

Examples: ka depa dila ni — "I hand you the key." ki damadesi, ko desi dopo — "the table, we sit around [it]." ka dela pasuza — "I carry [my] weapon" (reusing dela, carry, from the Motion Verbs batch).

Hapagi Communication & Cognition 53 roots · speaking, asking, thinking, knowing, learning

Two consonants carry almost the whole domain: ha (express) opens nearly every communication verb — hapo (say), hage (ask), havaga (shout), hano (command) — and the -ge process suffix does most of the cognitive work by turning an existing noun into its corresponding verb, exactly the pattern the Foundation's grammar already teaches: pagi (knowledge) + -ge = pagige (to know); bita (memory) + -ge = bitage (to remember); vivi (hope) + -ge = vivige (to hope); sigebu (fear) + -ge = sigebuge (to fear). Once a concept exists as a noun, turning it into "to experience/do that thing" almost never requires a new root — this is the reuse discipline from Appendix E paying off across an entire domain at once, not just a handful of words.

Examples: ka hage ni — "I ask you." ka pagige so ni bu-nesuki ka — "I know that you don't believe me" (stacking a cognition verb with the Foundation's subordinator so). ka bitabu — "I forget" (memory, nulled).

Sigilo Perception & Emotion 30 roots · the five senses, and naming feelings as qualities rather than events

Perception verbs all open on si (sense) — side (see), sihe (hear), sifade (smell), sila (touch) — the same shared-onset discipline as Animals' -va and Motion's de-. Emotion works differently, and deliberately so: the Foundation already lexicalised feelings as nouns (gilo love, bibu sadness, sigebu fear), so this batch doesn't invent competing emotion-verbs — it builds adjectives with -ke on those same nouns, because "I am sad" is a state you're in, not an action you perform, and Halu's grammar already has the right tool for states. bibuke (sad) is just bibu + the ordinary quality suffix, the identical mechanism gasi-ke (bright) has always used.

Examples: ka side ki — "I see it." ka bibuke — "I am sad." ka giki, sake ka gidasu nesu — "I am happy, because I realized the truth."

Nasu du Society, Law & Economy 52 roots · governance, justice, trade, work

This domain runs almost entirely on two roots reused as building blocks: na (boundary, already the language's negation particle) and su (law/pattern). Nearly every legal and institutional word is some combination of the two — nasu (law itself, "boundary made law"), nasudu (justice, "the domain of law"), namasu (equality, "the law of sameness"), hogasu (permission, "the law of what is open"). This isn't a coincidence of convenience: law is fundamentally the drawing and defending of boundaries, so a legal vocabulary built entirely on the boundary-root is more honest than one built from unrelated roots that happen to mean "court" or "judge." Economic terms lean the same way on lonasu (money, itself "bond+boundary+law" — an exchange bounded by shared law): lonasugi (profit, "money that has grown"), lonasubu (loss, "money, nulled"), lonasudama (bank, "money's home").

Examples: ki nasu, ki nabosu — "the law, [under] democracy." ka pasige, sake ka nodufo lonasu — "I work, because I need money." nasuko nesudo ki nasubu — "the judge judges the crime" (reusing nesudo, to judge, from the Cognition batch).

Sisu du Science, Technology & Mathematics 49 roots · disciplines, physics, devices, geometry

Every scientific discipline compounds on the newly-coined sisu ("sense, pursued to law") — sisugada (physics, the science of bodies), sisugefa (chemistry, the science of fire/transformation), sisukelo (biology, the science of the living) — the same shared-prefix discipline as si- for perception verbs and ha- for communication verbs. Devices compound on pasu (device, already lexicalised): pasuki (computer, "a device with its own felt identity"), pasukelo (robot, "a device given the appearance of life"). Mathematics reuses the numeral system directly rather than inventing new number-words: dacapo (triangle) and dacapu (square) are literally "a refined place with three/four [points]," using the digit words po (3) and pu (4) from Appendix G of the Foundation. Mathematics itself, jusu, is built on ju — the multiply-operator and place-value carry — plus su (law): the law of the numeral system, named from inside that same system.

Examples: ka sisu gada — "I [study] the science of the body" (physics, informally). ki dacapo, ki dacapu — "the triangle, the square." pasuki fusuge — "the computer [runs on] energy."

Sudu du Abstract & Philosophical 57 roots · the final batch — truth, self, cause, freedom, the absolute

This batch leans on a single grammatical move harder than any before it: -du (domain-abstract) and -bu (null-abstract), turning an already-lexicalised quality into "the domain where that quality governs" or "that quality, denied." nesu (true) becomes nesudu (truth); sifu (wise) becomes both sifudu (wisdom) and, with -bu instead, sifubu (ignorance) — the same root, read through domain versus negation, giving a matched pair for the price of one coinage. This is the oldest trick in the language, run at the highest level of abstraction it will go: Appendix E's "reuse before minting" applied not to physical objects anymore, but to the shape of thought itself.

A few pairs are worth sitting with. gimubu (necessity) is literally "impossibility, negated" — what cannot not be — a definition a logician would recognise on sight, arrived at without meaning to be clever, just by following the null-suffix where it led. nadusu (relativity) is "other" + "law": the law that a thing's nature is fixed only in relation to another thing — and nadusubu, "the absolute," is that very idea, denied. keloki (actuality) and gituki (potential) split on the same root pair (alive/possible) the way siva-pa and za-pa split on the same table row: two faces of one structure, not two unrelated words that happen to rhyme.

Examples: ka gilo nesudu — "I love truth." gimubu ne nadusubu — "necessity equals the absolute" (using the Foundation's arithmetic equals-particle outside of numbers, which the grammar permits — ne confirms any two things are the same, not only two quantities). ki gage, ki subu, ki suki — "transformation, chaos, order" — three states of the same underlying law, named in one breath.

Wa'lu tegu The Journey & the Freedom wa'lu (aether-phase) + te+gu (time+transcend) = the understanding that outlasts any one word learned

A person who opens the Foundation document for the first time meets Tier 1 the way every practitioner has met it: as arithmetic. Twelve consonants, five vowels, sixty positions, each one a fixed meaning multiplied against another fixed meaning. It is, at this stage, entirely mechanical — a table to be read, a cipher to be decoded, no different in spirit from memorising a multiplication grid. basa is earth because ba is return-to-physical and sa is distinguish-physical, and that is simply true the way 6×7 is simply 42. Nothing is felt yet. Everything is looked up.

Tier 2 is where the table stops being furniture and starts being weather. Polarity — the teaching that no quality exists as a fixed point, only as a position between two poles — arrives in the grammar at the exact moment it arrives in the phonology: diphthongs, sounds that never fully land on one vowel because they are always travelling toward another. A learner who has only ever pronounced monophthongs discovers, learning to say ai correctly, that the mouth itself now enacts the philosophy — a sensation becoming a felt state, spoken, not just defined. This is the tier where the dictionary lookup starts to feel less like consulting a table and more like recognising a face. y and w arrive because the ear can finally hear a spectrum instead of a point, and — not coincidentally — this is also where a learner typically stops asking "what does this word mean" and starts asking "what would the word for this be," which is a different kind of question entirely.

Tier 3 is Correspondence: the Hermetic claim that the law governing a cell's membrane is the same law governing a household's boundary is the same law governing a nation's border, one pattern read at three magnifications. Practically, this is where the seventh vowel and the fourteenth consonant complete the table — 98 syllables, its geometry finished — and where a learner notices, often for the first time without being told, that the Expansion vocabulary already built on Tier 1 and 2 has been doing exactly this the whole time. nasu (law) and lonasu (money) and nasudu (justice) are not three unrelated words that happen to share a syllable; they are the same boundary-law, read at three magnifications, exactly as Tier 3 predicts before the learner has consciously arrived at Tier 3. The map was already the territory. This is the tier where a fluent reader of this document stops being taught the principle and starts recognising it was operating on them three appendices ago.

Tier 4 is where r — present since Tier 3 only as an approximation, a sound reached for but not yet held — matures into its full trilled form. Nothing new is added to the table; the same 98 positions simply arrive at their complete, intended sound. This is the least dramatic tier to describe and the most telling to notice: growth, here, is not acquisition. It is the same material, finally settled into what it always was. A practitioner at this stage typically finds they can coin a word correctly on the first attempt more often than not — not because they have memorised more of the dictionary, but because the shape of correct coinage has become as automatic as the shape of a native speaker's grammar.

Tier 5 adds no new sound at all. It is only this: the recursive return, the bounded individual and the whole table seen as the same statement from two sides. A word and the table it came from stop feeling like two different things — the word was always just the table, spoken once, in one direction, for one purpose. This is not a metaphor for fluency. It is what fluency in a table-built language actually is.

The Freedom — Why 902 Words Was Never the Real Number
Every word in both documents — all 902 of them — is derived, not declared. That single fact is what "relativity" means here, in the sense meant for this closing: no syllable in Halu carries meaning in isolation. va is not "resonate-physical" as an absolute, free-floating fact; it is resonate-physical relative to the row it sits in, the vowel-domain it closes, the tier its consonant belongs to, and the position it occupies in the word being built. Move it, and its contribution to the meaning moves with it — exactly the way a value in relativity is never absolute, only ever measured against a frame. This is what makes the dictionary a cache and not a ceiling: a person who has internalised the relations — force × domain, tier × register, habitat × body-plan, growth-form × differentiator — owns the same generative power used to build every word in this document, including the ones written this afternoon. Appendix E of the Foundation calls this "Naming the Unknown" and teaches it as a method in four steps. Everything built across seven batches of this Expansion is the proof that the method scales: 732 new roots, and not one of them required a change to the rules that were already there. The table did not get bigger. Only the words drawn from it did.

So the honest number was never 902, or 1,000, or any fixed count a document could claim to have finished. The honest number is sixty times five times however many tiers a given practitioner has climbed — a space, not a list. What these two documents actually hand a reader is not a vocabulary to memorise but a permission, earned the moment the rules are understood well enough to be trusted: coin the word your sentence needs, check it against nothing but the table and the words that already exist, and if it survives that check, it was always a Halu word — this document simply hadn't written it down yet.
